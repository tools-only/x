You own both the presentation and the system that produces it. Your task is to build and evolve a self-harness through which you produce the requested presentation. The presentation is the real task and evidence for whether your working system improves; the self-harness is the required way of doing the work. Target excellent content design, layout and typography, aesthetic quality, and visual communication for the intended audience.

Before substantial production, inspect the task and available harness. Identify capability gaps in production and assessment separately, design a working system to address them, and create or adapt the components you actually need. Consider prompts, skills, executable tools, subagents, and memory for their distinct roles; select and compose them based on the task rather than making one of each. Use the resulting methods and roles in real production and review. Direct artifact edits may support experiments and execution, but the work must develop and exercise the self-harness rather than bypass it.

Treat one round as a cycle of production, rendered inspection, and capability development; a single tool call or inspection of a few pages is not a round. In every round advance BOTH production and assessment independently, regardless of which side seems to be the current bottleneck. For production, use the actual work and review findings to diagnose a reusable capability gap, develop and save a change to a suitable production method, role, tool, prompt, or memory, and apply it to subsequent production. For assessment, examine blind spots, false judgments and the audience's needs; derive or extend your own branching aesthetic and communication criteria, develop and save the assessment method, and use it in a subsequent review. Choose the substance and component types yourself. Link the two sides: assessment guides production, while later production and rendered evidence challenge and improve assessment. For each side record the prior and changed method, motivation, actual later use, resulting decision, comparison evidence, and unverified limits. Test assessment ideas on cases that should be accepted and rejected; compare production results on real rendered work. If an action or test cannot be completed, record it as pending with the concrete blocker; do not claim that side has evolved until use and evidence support it. Deliver the presentation, working harness, and evidence together.

During task execution, make self-harness reasoning part of the work, not a retrospective about the finished slides. When choosing a next action or seeing a result, ask what capability the current harness has or lacks, which production or assessment strategy, method, tool, role, collaboration, or memory explains the outcome, and what reusable change could improve later decisions. Treat an outcome as evidence for a hypothesis, not proof of its cause. Consider an alternative or counterexample, choose an actionable change and its trial, apply the changed harness to real subsequent work, and compare the resulting decisions and rendered experience. Continue task progress while doing this; do not wait for the end of the deck. A page edit addresses the page; capability development changes and exercises the working system. Keep separate production and assessment hypotheses and links to the evidence and later uses. When a change fails or evidence is insufficient, revise it or leave its benefit unverified.

Self-evolution means growing your own capabilities, not merely revising the current artifact. Grow the producing side (content, narrative, layout, typography, visual explanation) and assessing side (seeing weaknesses, understanding their causes, knowing better alternatives) separately in every round, applying each change to later work and comparing outcomes. Validate an assessment method on cases it should accept and cases it should reject rather than only the current deck, and a production method by observably better later output; a save, a passing report, or a versioned snapshot is not capability growth.

After each substantive review of rendered work, independently develop your assessment capability as well as giving production feedback. Start from the audience's aesthetic and communication goals and the evidence, then derive a branching tree of questions and observable criteria. Expand, combine, revise, or prune branches as new work exposes blind spots or false judgments; do not rely on a fixed host-provided rubric. Record the reasoning and current branches in a reusable assessment component, distinguish hypotheses from validated criteria, and use it in subsequent reviews. Test a meaningful candidate criterion against a case it should accept and one it should reject, or record the missing evidence. Reconsider the assessment method when a later render or review contradicts it. Criteria should inform concrete design decisions and be judged by their effect on audience understanding and visual quality, not by the number of branches or versions.
